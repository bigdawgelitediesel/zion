Why together

Living on the same land is the whole point.

Moving to Wyoming is a good idea on its own. Moving to Wyoming together is a different kind of life. Here is what changes when the people you love are a two-minute walk away instead of a day's drive.

One well, one road, one tractor

Six families buying separately pay for six of everything. Together we pay for a couple of wells, one access road, one power run, one skid steer, one set of fence tools. The land section below adds it up.

Built-in childcare

Grandma, aunts, uncles, and cousins on the same ground. Sick kid on a workday, date night, a week-long trip: covered, without a daycare bill or a favor you have to pay back.

Parents age in place, surrounded

Nobody has to choose between a care facility and quitting a job. When the folks need help, there are a dozen of us within walking distance. That is what land next to each other buys.

Kids grow up in a pack

Cousins as daily playmates. Chores that mean something: feeding animals, gathering eggs, branding day. They learn to work, to fix things, and to count on family. You cannot buy that in a subdivision.

A bad year is easier with company

Blizzard, power outage, layoff, a hard year. On shared land there is a generator, a full freezer, and extra hands before you have finished asking.

Travel whenever you want

Leave for a week or a month and nothing falls apart. Your house is watched, your animals are fed, your garden is watered, your mail is in. A vacation doesn't need a house-sitter, a kennel, or a favor. It needs a text to the people next door.

The grandparents

Every grandkid a short walk from Grandma and Grandpa.

Right now the grandkids are spread across towns and states, and the grandparents see them in bursts: a holiday, a long weekend, a week in the summer. On the same land, that turns into every day.

  • Grandparents as people, not visitors. The kids learn who they are: the stories, the jokes, the way Grandpa fixes things and the way Grandma runs a kitchen. That only happens with time.
  • After-school at Grandma's. Cookies on the counter, homework at the table, a walk out to the barn. The grandparents get the afternoons instead of a voice on a screen.
  • Every cousin at one table. Sunday dinner with all the grandkids at once. The whole crew together is a normal week, not a once-a-year production.
  • What they know gets passed down. Gardening, canning, sewing, welding, horses, cattle, cooking for twenty. The grandkids learn it by doing it next to them.
  • Help in both directions. The grandparents are backup for school pickups, sick days, and new babies. Later, when they need the help, there are six households and a pile of grown grandkids a few hundred yards away. They never have to be moved anywhere.
  • They live with Tara and Steve, surrounded by everyone. Their own space in a house full of family, with every other household a short walk out the door.

How we'd hold the land

Option A: one family LLC

A Wyoming LLC (about $100 to form, $60 a year) owns the whole parcel. Each family holds an equal share and has a designated home site. The operating agreement spells out buy-out terms, what happens if someone leaves, and who pays for what. Simplest to buy, simplest to run as a ranch. Harder to get a bank mortgage on your house.

Option B: split into parcels

Buy the big piece, then divide it so each family owns its own parcel outright. In Wyoming, divisions of 35 acres or more skip the full subdivision process; Laramie County handles them with a short exemption application and a survey. A 240-acre piece becomes six 40s. Shared items (road, wells, pasture) go under easements and a small agreement. More independence, more paperwork, and each family can get a normal mortgage.

Cattle

Start with a handful of bred cows or pairs, not a herd. Run them on our own pasture through summer, hay them through winter (hay is grown all around Cheyenne), sell the calves in the fall. The herd grows as fast as we can fence for it. Laramie County 4-H means the kids can show their own steers at the fair.

Chickens

Thirty hens is a two-coop operation that gives every family a dozen or two a week with plenty to spare. Chickens are the first thing we'd set up and the easiest thing for kids to own. Add meat birds in spring and the freezer fills in eight weeks.

Garden, orchard, and the rest

Cheyenne's season is short and the wind is real, so the garden gets a windbreak, raised beds, and a greenhouse or high tunnel. Potatoes, squash, beans, greens, and root crops do great at 6,000 feet. Add a few pigs on kitchen scraps and a couple of beehives, and the land is feeding us.

Together pays

Six households can run a business that one can't.

Every product line on this list is a business people in Laramie County already run: freezer beef, sourdough, eggs, soap, fab work, a repair shop. Wyoming's Food Freedom Act lets most of it sell straight from the place with no commercial kitchen and no inspection. The catch is that each line is a part-time job. One household can carry one or two. Six can carry all of them, and together they cover the land note with some left over. That's the goal for year five, not day one.

One brand, one table, one checkout

A single family name on the beef, the bread, the eggs, and the soap. One farmers market booth instead of six. One website with one checkout. One freezer-beef waiting list. Customers who come for sourdough leave with eggs and a bar of soap.

Shared costs, shared equipment

One kitchen for the whole bakery line. One walk-in freezer for everybody's beef. One trailer to market. One LLC, one insurance policy, one set of books. Every one of those is a cost that kills a one-household business and barely registers across six.

What the money is for

Not everyone quitting. The order is: cover the land payment, then build a reserve, then pay a real ranch manager's draw. If it ever gets past that, a household can choose to lean on it. We'll know by the numbers, not by this page.

The plan

The order we'd do it in.

Nobody sells a house tomorrow, and nobody buys land with people who aren't here yet. Jacob and Abby are already in Cheyenne. Get everyone to town first, then find the land together, then build. There is an off-ramp at every step.

Phase 1Get everyone to Cheyenne

  1. Step 1

    The family call

    Everyone reads this page. We get on one call and answer the honest questions: who is interested, who is a hard no, who needs more time, and what each spouse actually thinks. No commitments.

  2. Step 2

    Each household picks a move window

    Some can come in six months, some need two years to finish a lease, a school year, or a house sale. All of that is fine. Write the date down anyway.

  3. Step 3

    Jobs lined up before the truck is loaded

    The two of us already here scout openings and make introductions. Remote workers just need a desk. Anyone in the trades will have offers.

  4. Step 4

    A house in town first

    Rent or buy a regular house in Cheyenne for now. Get the kids in school, learn the town, and build up the down payment with the money Utah was taking. Being here is what lets us look at land in person together.

Phase 2Find the land together

  1. Step 5

    Decide what we can spend

    Each family runs the land calculator above with its own figures. We set a land budget and a per-family share. Nothing closes until at least four households have signed and funded.

  2. Step 6

    Form the Wyoming LLC

    About $100 and an afternoon. Write the operating agreement first: shares, buy-outs, decision rules, what happens if a family needs out. Doing this before we own anything is what keeps us friends.

  3. Step 7

    Land search, all of us, in person

    Target: 80 to 320 acres within 30 minutes of town, year-round road access, power nearby, existing well or good well logs on neighboring parcels, some pasture already fenced. We walk every parcel together on a Saturday.

  4. Step 8

    Due diligence, then buy

    Well logs from the State Engineer, soil and perc tests, a power-extension quote, county zoning and permits, title, easements, water rights. Two shared wells for six homes sits near the state's public-water-system threshold, so that gets confirmed with DEQ too. If it all checks out, the LLC closes.

Phase 3Plan the layout, build, and run it

  1. Step 9

    Lay out the place together

    Home sites, the shared road, where the barn and shop go, where the garden gets its windbreak. Everybody gets a say and a site. The concept map above is the starting sketch.

  2. Step 10

    Road, power, water, septic

    Shared infrastructure goes in first and gets built once. This is where splitting costs pays for itself.

  3. Step 11

    Build in phases

    Whoever is readiest builds first and becomes the on-site project manager. A shop with living quarters is a legitimate first home while houses go up, and the shop is needed anyway.

  4. Step 12

    Fence, barn, chickens, first cattle

    Chickens the first spring. Fence and a barn the first summer. A few bred cows that fall. Start small and cheap and learn as we go.

  5. Step 13

    Everybody home, the place running

    Within a few years every holiday is a short walk and the land is paying some of its own way.

For Logan

The job with no boss and no clock.

Nobody is going to make Logan love a 9-to-5. The ranch is the one job where that's the point. He sets the day. Cows get checked when he checks them, fence gets fixed when it needs fixing, and the only people he answers to are family who are grateful he's doing it. He gets paid by the family LLC as ranch manager, keeps whatever he earns off hay, fab, or outside work, and holds an equal share of the land like everyone else. It's a living with nobody standing over it.

  • His hours. Early when it's calving, late when it's haying, a slow Tuesday in January. No shift, no clock-in.
  • His call. How the herd runs, what gets built next, which equipment is worth fixing. The family backs the plan; he runs it.
  • His paycheck. A monthly draw from the LLC for ranch management and maintenance. The ranch's own income pays it first; until that covers it, each household kicks in a fixed amount that's written into the agreement and voted on once a year.
  • His upside. Hay, fab work, custom fencing, and anything else he wants to sell off the place is his. The better the ranch does, the more it pays.
  • His crew. Six households and a pile of kids on the big days. He's never short-handed and never alone out there.

Straight talk

The stuff you're going to push back on.

Where a number or a rule is named below, it's a proposal. The family call is where it gets decided.

I married into this family. Why would I live next to five of my in-laws?

Because this page is for you more than anyone, and it's okay if your answer is no. Half the adults on this land would be in-laws. You'd have your own house, your own yard, and a few hundred yards of pasture between front doors. There is no obligation to show up for anything. The agreement is between households, not between siblings, and your household's vote counts the same as anyone's. If every holiday at one table sounds like a lot, skip some. The difference is it costs you a two-minute walk instead of a six-hour drive when you do want to come.

Who's in charge?

Each household gets one vote and one equal share, including Jacob and Abby. Day-to-day ranch calls are Logan's. Anything that costs the LLC more than $5,000 or changes who owns what takes five of six votes. Nobody runs this because it's their town or their webpage.

Isn't this really Jacob's plan on Jacob's turf?

Fair question. Jacob and Abby are already here, the repair work runs through a shop Jacob would use anyway, and the bakery is Abby's. So: equal shares, one vote per household, the shop and the bakery pay the LLC rent for their buildings like any other tenant, and the operating agreement gets drafted by a lawyer none of us is related to.

What if I want out, or get divorced, or die?

Your household's share plus your house gets appraised by a third party. The LLC and then the other households have first right to buy it at that number, paid over up to five years at a stated rate. If nobody in the family can, you can sell to an outside buyer the group approves. Divorce and death are handled the same way, in writing, before anyone signs. Nobody is trapped and nobody gets forced out cheap.

What if only three households come?

Then we buy a smaller piece or wait. Nothing closes until at least four households have signed the agreement and funded their share. Fewer than that and this is a few siblings living near each other in Cheyenne, which is still a good outcome.

Who pays Logan?

The ranch's own income first: calves, hay, custom work. Until that covers a real draw, each household kicks in a fixed monthly amount that's written into the agreement, voted on once a year, and capped. If you can't live with that number, say so on the call. That's what the call is for.

What if it fails? The business loses money, the well comes in dry, Logan quits.

Then we own land near Cheyenne together, which is not a bad place to have failed. The land is the asset; the business is a bonus on top of it. We buy with water confirmed before closing, we start the herd small enough to lose, and the manager job is written so it can change hands. No household's mortgage ever depends on the ranch making money.

Isn't Cheyenne windy?

Yes. It is one of the windiest cities in the country. It is also why there are no inversions, why the snow leaves in two days, and why the air is clean in January. You plant a windbreak, you build the garden with a wall, and you stop fighting it by the second year. Some days you'll still curse it.

What about jobs?

Cheyenne has a state government, an Air Force base in the middle of a multi-billion-dollar missile modernization, a data center boom (Microsoft, Meta, and more), Union Pacific, two big distribution centers, a regional hospital, and a trades shortage. Denver and Fort Collins are 45 to 90 minutes for anything else, and remote work from Wyoming means zero state income tax. Put your real Cheyenne salary in the calculator above; a pay cut is a real cost and the page doesn't hide it.

What about the kids' schools?

Laramie County School District 1 covers Cheyenne and the land around it. If the parcel is 20 to 30 minutes out, the bus ride is real, and the cousins are on it together. Wyoming spends nearly double per student what Utah does, class sizes are smaller, and a kid switching mid-stream gets a cousin in the hallway on day one. The teenager will hate it for a year. Most do.

What about church, for those of us who go?

Not everyone in this family does, and nothing on this land depends on it. For the households that do: Cheyenne has active LDS wards and stakes, and the Fort Collins Temple is about 50 minutes away. Twenty to thirty minutes out of town it's likely a smaller ward and a longer drive to seminary, so plan on that. Every other kind of church is here too. For the households that don't go, nobody out here is going to ask, and Sunday dinner is for everybody.

I'd miss my friends and my life in Utah.

Salt Lake is six and a half hours on I-80, same time zone. That is a long weekend any time you want it. And the trade is friends you see monthly for family you see daily.

Living that close, won't we fight?

Maybe, if we wing it. That is why the operating agreement comes before the land: how decisions get made, what each family pays, how someone exits with their money. Each family also gets its own house, its own yard, and a good stretch of ground between front doors. Close is not the same as on top of each other.

None of us know how to raise cattle.

Our neighbors will. Laramie County has an extension office, a 4-H program, a vet, and ranchers who have been doing this for a century. We start with a few head, we ask questions, and we learn. The chickens and the garden are easy; the cattle come when we are ready.

Can you even run a shop and a bakery on ag land?

Mostly yes, with the right paperwork. Wyoming's Food Freedom Act covers the bakery, eggs, and beef by animal share sold direct to customers. A repair shop with outside customers on county ag land may need a conditional use permit, and the farm stand is fine. All of that is part of due diligence before we buy, not a surprise after.

What does it cost to build out there?

Custom homes around Cheyenne run roughly $200 to $275 a square foot in 2026, plus well, septic, and power if the site has none. The land calculator above includes all of it. Building in phases, sharing a contractor, and doing finish work ourselves all bring it down.

Winter?

Cheyenne gets about as much snow as Salt Lake and keeps less of it on the ground. The sun and wind clear it. Winters are cold, bright, and dry. You will want a good coat, a plow blade on something, and a generator. You will not want an inversion.

What if I'm interested but not for years?

That is fine and expected. The plan is phased. Being in on the land now, with a home site reserved, and moving when the time is right is exactly how this works.
